Too Fast to Track

CaseFort Monmouth, NJ · Posted Nov 20, 2026

An Army radar locked onto something too fast for the set to follow, and 25 minutes later two Air Force pilots dove after a silver disc.
Point Pleasant, New Jersey. The Army Signal Corps radar school at Fort Monmouth and the sky over Sandy Hook, 11:10 AM, September 10, 1951.

A student operator was demonstrating an AN/MPG-1 radar for a group of visiting officers. When he switched to automatic tracking, he picked up a low target moving along the coast so fast the set could not keep up, about 700 miles per hour. He had to follow it by hand until it was gone.

At 11:35 AM, Lt. Wilbert Rogers and Major Ballard were flying a T-33 jet trainer at 20,000 feet over Point Pleasant when Rogers saw a dull silver, flat, disc shaped object far below, 30 to 50 feet across, heading toward Sandy Hook. He nosed down and banked after it. The object banked too, showing its round flat edge, then turned out to sea and pulled away even as he pushed the jet to 550 mph. The next day, radar sets at the fort tracked more fast targets that nobody could name, and the reports landed at the Pentagon.

The debate: the Air Force investigators blamed the radar track on a student's mistake and the pilots' disc on two weather balloons released that morning from the Evans Signal Laboratory nearby. The pilots said the thing banked, turned and outran a jet, which is not something a balloon does.

Could a balloon outrun a jet?

It Fell In Behind

CaseAlbuquerque, NM · Posted Nov 20, 2026

An egg shaped object with a single white light came down over the runways of a nuclear weapons base, and then radar watched it fall in behind a military plane.
Albuquerque, New Mexico. Kirtland Air Force Base, about 10:45 PM, November 4, 1957, the night after Sputnik 2 went up.

Two civilian control tower operators, R.M. Kaser and E.G. Brink, saw a lighted object come down steeply at the east end of the runway. It left the flight line and crossed the runways and taxiways at less than 100 feet, heading toward the tower. Through binoculars it had no wings, no tail and no fuselage, just an egg shape standing on end, 15 to 20 feet tall, "the size of an automobile standing on its nose," with one white light at its base. It hovered near a restricted area for about a minute, then climbed steeply into the overcast.

The tower called the base radar unit. Radar showed a target heading east, then turning south at very high speed toward the Albuquerque radio range station, where it circled for several minutes. Then it came back toward Kirtland and took up a position right behind an Air Force C-46 that had just taken off, and stayed there until both left radar range.

The debate: the Air Force and later the Condon Report decided it was a small, powerful private plane with no flight plan whose lost pilot tried to land at the wrong airport, then pulled up when he saw the lit restricted area. The radar operator said the return looked like a normal aircraft. Both tower men said it looked nothing like a plane.

Would a lost pilot really hover over a nuclear base?
